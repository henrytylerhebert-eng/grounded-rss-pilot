Stop Chasing Soreness: What Progress Actually Looks Like

Feeling wrecked after every session isn't a badge of honor — and it's probably slowing you down. Here's how to tell real progress from just being tired.

TH
Tyler Hebert  ·  Coach, Grounded Fitness
Aug 20, 2026 6 min read

If you judge a workout by how sore you are the next day, you're measuring the wrong thing. Soreness tells you something was unfamiliar — not that it was effective.

At Grounded, we get some version of this question every week: "Coach, I wasn't that sore — did I even do enough?" It's one of the most stubborn myths in fitness, and it quietly holds people back. Let's dig into what's actually happening in your body, and what to track instead.

What Soreness Really Measures

Delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS) shows up most when you do something new, or emphasize the lowering phase of a lift. That's why your first week back always hurts the most — and why the same workout barely touches you a month later, even though you're fitter. Adaptation is the goal, and adaptation means less soreness over time, not more.

Train to get better, not to get destroyed. Consistency beats intensity, one good hour at a time. — Grounded Coaching Philosophy

Four Signals That Actually Track Progress

Instead of chasing wreckage, watch these. They're boring, they're measurable, and they're honest:

  • Load over time. Same movement, more weight or reps than a month ago.
  • Recovery speed. Your heart rate settles faster between efforts.
  • Consistency. Sessions per week you actually complete.
  • Everyday capacity. Stairs, groceries, playing with your kids — it all feels easier.
